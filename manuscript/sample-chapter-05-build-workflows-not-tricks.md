# Sample Chapter — Build Workflows, Not Tricks

## Chapter 5. Build Workflows, Not Tricks

A trick works once.

A workflow works on Tuesday, next month, and after the model changes.

Chapter 5 turns the brief into a repeatable operating path. The point is durability, not one better answer.

People love prompt tricks because tricks feel like leverage. A clever instruction can produce a better draft, a sharper summary, or a cleaner structure in one moment. But isolated wins do not create durable capability. They create dependency on whatever happens to work today.

High-agency work needs something sturdier: a way to gather inputs, transform them, review them, decide on them, and record what happened so the next cycle starts from a better place.

Used well, AI helps build that path. Used badly, it becomes a stunt engine: the person is always looking for the next clever prompt, the next shortcut, the next tactic that might rescue a mediocre process. The output may look impressive once. The process remains fragile.

If the workflow cannot survive a busy day, a different teammate, a changed deadline, or a new model version, then the system is not strong enough yet. It is just a trick with better packaging.

## Why tricks fail

Tricks are tempting because they feel immediate. But they fail in predictable ways.

### 1. They solve only the moment
A trick can improve one output without improving the process that produced it. The next time the task appears, the same confusion returns.

### 2. They do not travel well
If the best result depends on the exact phrasing one person discovered on one afternoon, the value is brittle. It does not scale across people, teams, or use cases.

### 3. They hide the real structure of the job
Tricks often focus on the surface request. They do not force the user to define inputs, checkpoints, ownership, or review criteria.

### 4. They make quality hard to compare
Without a repeatable process, it is difficult to know whether the work improved because the workflow improved or because the model happened to produce a better answer that day.

### 5. They weaken accountability
If the process is just “ask the model until it sounds right,” nobody can tell where judgment lives or who owns the result.

That is why the move from tricks to workflows matters. The goal is not to use AI more often. The goal is to use it inside a process that can be understood, repeated, and improved.

## What a workflow actually is

A workflow is a repeatable sequence for turning a known kind of input into a known kind of output with known checkpoints in between.

A good AI-assisted workflow usually has five parts:

1. **Inputs** — what comes in
2. **Transformation** — what the system does with it
3. **Review** — what gets checked and by whom
4. **Decision** — what gets approved, revised, or shipped
5. **Record** — what gets remembered for next time

If one of those parts is missing, the process is still vulnerable.

### Inputs: what the workflow needs

Inputs are the raw materials. They might be notes, source documents, data, customer feedback, meeting transcripts, past examples, or a combination of those things.

The important question is not just “What do I have?” It is “What should be allowed into the workflow, and in what form?”

If the input is messy, the workflow should say how messy is acceptable.
If the input is incomplete, the workflow should say what can wait and what cannot.
If the input is sensitive, the workflow should say who can touch it.

Concrete rule: a workflow without input standards will spend its life cleaning up avoidable noise.
### Transformation: what happens to the inputs

Transformation is where AI usually enters the picture.

This may include:
- summarizing,
- classifying,
- comparing,
- restructuring,
- drafting,
- compressing,
- or surfacing likely gaps and objections.

The key question is not whether AI can do the transformation. It usually can do some of it. The question is whether that step is clearly bounded.

What is AI supposed to do first?
What is it not supposed to decide?
What output format should it produce?
What should remain human-authored?

Concrete rule: if the transformation step is not explicit, the model will quietly fill in the missing structure for you.

### Review: what must be checked

Review is where workflows become trustworthy.

AI output should almost never go straight from generation to use when the work matters. Something needs to verify the result:
- factual accuracy,
- completeness,
- tone,
- decision readiness,
- audience fit,
- compliance with constraints,
- or alignment with the original brief.

The review stage is where the workflow earns its credibility.

Concrete rule: if nobody is clearly responsible for review, the workflow is pretending to be more reliable than it is.

### Decision: who signs off

Many processes collapse because the output looks finished while the decision remains ambiguous.

Who accepts the draft?
Who decides it is good enough?
Who owns the tradeoff?
Who is allowed to move it forward?

This is where the human loop matters, but not every workflow needs the same human loop. Chapter 6 will get more precise about delegation and ownership. For now, the point is simpler: a workflow must specify where the real decision lives.

Concrete rule: if no one can point to the final owner, the workflow is not complete.

### Record: what gets kept

A workflow improves only if the next cycle can learn from the last one.

That means the process should preserve:
- the prompt or template that worked,
- the input bundle used,
- the review notes,
- the final version,
- and the reason changes were made.

Without a record, the same lessons have to be rediscovered every time.

Concrete rule: if the workflow produces no memory, it produces more effort than leverage.

## The part tricks usually miss: provenance, review gates, and owner trails

A workflow is stronger than a trick because it leaves a trail behind it. You can inspect where the input came from, where review actually happened, and who had the authority to move the work across the final boundary. That is what keeps AI-assisted work honest when polish arrives faster than judgment.

## How to design a reusable workflow

You do not need a giant automation project to begin.

Start with one recurring task. One. The goal is not to redesign everything at once. The goal is to build a process that survives repetition.

To make the rest of the chapter concrete, keep one recurring task in view: a weekly client update built from scattered notes, blockers, decisions, and stakeholder concerns. The same structure also works for a project lead or manager, but the client-facing version keeps the workflow broad enough to travel.

The same structure also works for a solo contributor. A writer preparing a weekly newsletter can collect source links, ask AI for candidate outlines, review for accuracy and voice, choose the best opening, and keep the final version plus the note that explains why it worked.

### Step 1. Pick a task that repeats

Choose a task that appears regularly enough to justify structure.

It might be:
- a weekly update,
- a recurring brief,
- a standard recommendation note,
- a follow-up after meetings,
- a status summary,
- or an internal review packet.

If the task only happens once, it may not be worth formalizing yet. If it happens every week or every month, it probably is.

### Step 2. Map the current path

Before adding AI, write down how the work happens now.

Ask:
- Where does the input come from?
- Who assembles it?
- Where does friction appear?
- What does the first draft usually miss?
- Who checks it?
- What usually causes rework?

This matters because the workflow should be built around the real process, not the idealized one.
### Step 3. Define the output standard

A workflow needs a target shape.

What counts as a good first pass?
What counts as a good final pass?
What must never be missing?

If the output can vary without limit, the workflow will drift.

Concrete rule: the more repeatable the task, the more precise the standard should be.

### Step 4. Decide where AI belongs

This is where many people go wrong. They start by asking what AI can do, not what the process needs.

Ask instead:
- Should AI prepare the raw material?
- Should it draft a structure?
- Should it compress or compare inputs?
- Should it surface likely objections?
- Should it generate a first version for review?

Then ask the harder question:
- What should still be done by a human before the result can move forward?

AI should fill the right gap in the workflow, not occupy the whole workflow.

### Step 5. Build the review gate

Every workflow needs a checkpoint that is more than a vibe check.

The review gate should use a small set of questions:
- Did it solve the right problem?
- Did it stay inside the constraints?
- Is the evidence strong enough?
- Does the tone fit the audience?
- Is anything important missing?

If the answers are not obvious, the workflow needs a better gate.

### Step 6. Run one cycle and refine

The first version of a workflow will not be the final version.

That is fine. The point is to run the task once, note what broke, and improve the process.

Ask:
- What slowed us down?
- What was overexplained?
- What was still too vague?
- Where did AI help?
- Where did it add cleanup?

Good workflows get better because they are used. They are not designed perfectly in advance.

## A representative scenario: the weekly client update

Imagine an independent consultant who sends a weekly client update every Friday.

Before building a workflow, the update happens like this:

On Thursday night, the consultant gathers scattered notes, digs through messages, asks AI to “clean this up,” gets a neat draft, edits it lightly, and sends it out. The result is usually acceptable. It is also inconsistent.

Sometimes the update overstates progress.
Sometimes it buries risk.
Sometimes it leaves out an important decision.
Sometimes it reflects the source material too loosely.

The problem is not that AI was used.
The problem is that AI was used without a workflow.

Now imagine the same task with an actual process.

### Inputs
Every week, the consultant collects:
- the three most important project notes,
- any blocked items,
- one or two metrics,
- open decisions,
- and any client concerns worth surfacing.

### Transformation
AI turns that material into a first-pass structure:
- wins,
- risks,
- decisions needed,
- and next steps.

### Review
The consultant checks:
- whether the risks are named honestly,
- whether the status matches the evidence,
- whether any claim is too strong,
- and whether the update is safe for the actual client.

### Decision
The consultant decides what will be sent, what must be softened, and what needs a separate follow-up conversation.

### Record
The final update and the review notes are kept so next Friday starts with a better template.

The team also keeps a short provenance trail:
- which inputs were used,
- which risk line required human rewriting,
- who cleared the final wording,
- and what changed between the AI draft and the shipped version.

What changed is not just efficiency.

The consultant now has a system that can survive a busy week, a change in tempo, or a substitute reviewer. The workflow no longer depends on memory alone or on one clever prompt.

That is the real gain.

The same pattern applies to many recurring tasks:
- a project brief,
- a stakeholder recap,
- a research summary,
- a meeting follow-up,
- a client memo,
- a recurring client update.

The details change. The architecture stays the same.

A creator- or individual-contributor version looks similar. A freelance designer or independent consultant may run the same workflow each Friday: gather client notes, flag open approvals, summarize deliverable risks, review tone before sending, and keep a record of what changed so the next update does not start from scratch. The work is smaller in scale than a larger team brief, but the architecture is the same: inputs, transformation, review, decision, and record.

## Where workflows go wrong

A workflow can still fail, even if it is repeatable.

### 1. It becomes too elaborate
The process gets so complicated that the work slows down more than it improves.

### 2. It automates the wrong layer
The system speeds up the part that should still be human-owned and leaves the actual bottleneck untouched.

### 3. It has no feedback loop
The workflow runs, but nobody records what should change next time.

### 4. It hides a single point of failure
Only one person knows how the process really works, which means the workflow is not actually durable.

### 5. It treats the model as the workflow
This is the biggest mistake. The model is one component. The workflow is the whole system around it.

Concrete rule: if a workflow only works when one person remembers every exception, it is not a workflow yet.

## A practical checklist for building workflows, not tricks

Before building:
- What recurring task are we trying to improve?
- What is the real output standard?
- What input material is required?
- Where does the current process break down?
- What must remain human-owned?

While building:
- What is the exact role of AI in this step?
- What should the model produce?
- What should it not decide?
- Where is the review gate?
- Who is the final owner?

After one cycle:
- Did the workflow save time?
- Did it improve quality?
- Did it create hidden cleanup?
- Did it make judgment clearer or blurrier?
- What should change before the next run?

## The deeper point

Prompt tricks are attractive because they make the work feel clever.

Workflows are attractive because they make the work more reliable.

That difference matters more as AI becomes normal. A clever prompt may help once, but a workflow creates an engine for repeated quality. It turns a one-off interaction into a process that can be learned, shared, reviewed, and improved.

That is the real professional advantage.

Not a bigger library of hacks.
Not a secret sentence that beats the model.
Not a collection of clever shortcuts that only one person understands.

The advantage is a process that survives pressure.

Build the thing once.
Use it again.
Check where it fails.
Improve it.

That is how AI becomes part of the operating model instead of a novelty layer on top of the work.

Once the workflow exists, the next question is where each step should live: what can be delegated, what must be supervised, and what stays unmistakably human-owned. That is Chapter 6.
