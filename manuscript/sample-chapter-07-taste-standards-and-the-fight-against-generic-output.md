# Sample Chapter — Taste, Standards, and the Fight Against Generic Output

## Chapter 7. Taste, Standards, and the Fight Against Generic Output

Generic output used to be easier to ignore.

Part II ends here: brief, workflow, ownership, then standards strong enough to stop plausible but generic output from quietly becoming acceptable.

AI can produce language, structure, summaries, option sets, and polished first passes quickly enough that many professionals encounter the finished surface before they have defined what quality should be. The result is not always obviously bad. Often it is competent enough to survive, clean enough to circulate, and too weak to carry much weight.

That is the real danger. The main problem with generic output is not that it is ugly. The problem is that it quietly lowers the standard of what people are willing to accept. If a team sees smooth language, tidy bullets, and a respectable structure, it becomes easy to confuse presentability with quality. The work moves forward because it looks complete, not because it has been judged carefully.

Before you can use AI well in writing, research, planning, or coordination, you need a quality-control layer. You need a way to tell the difference between an output that is merely plausible and one that is actually strong enough to keep.

That method is what this chapter calls taste and standards.

Taste, in professional work, is a review system. It is the ability to notice when something is off even if it is technically acceptable. It is the discipline of deciding what counts as good before volume arrives.

## Why AI amplifies the average

AI is useful partly because it is very good at producing patterns that usually work.

That is also why it so easily amplifies the average.

In many workflows, the model is drawing from common structures, common phrasings, common transitions, common explanations, and common ways of sounding professionally safe. That makes it strong at producing something legible quickly. It also means that unless the human operator adds sharper criteria, the output drifts toward what is broadly acceptable rather than what is especially good.

This matters in every domain the rest of the book will cover.

In writing, the average becomes fluent but forgettable language.  
In research, it becomes summaries that sound informed without making the strength of evidence visible.  
In planning, it becomes balanced options that avoid naming the real tradeoff.  
In coordination, it becomes polished recaps that create the appearance of alignment without the burden of real clarity.

The pattern is the same.

When generation gets easier, selection gets harder.

Not because there are fewer good options, but because there are many more acceptable-looking weak ones. A professional who cannot review decisively gets buried under plausible output. A professional with standards can use the same abundance as leverage.

So the right question is not, “How do I make the model more creative?”

The better question is, “What review system will stop average output from quietly becoming my standard?”

## Taste is not a mood. It is a set of operating decisions.

In actual work, taste shows up in repeated decisions:
- what gets cut,
- what gets sharpened,
- what feels too vague,
- what sounds borrowed,
- what overstates certainty,
- what lacks tension,
- what is technically fine but still weak,
- and what does not fit the seriousness of the job.

Taste is the mechanism that turns standards into editorial action.

That is why taste and standards belong together. Standards tell you what the work must accomplish. Taste helps you detect when the work has missed the mark even if it has met the formal minimum.

A useful way to think about the relationship is this:

- **Standards** answer: what does good require here?  
- **Taste** answers: can I tell when the work still falls short?  
- **Review discipline** answers: what do I do about it before this ships?

Without standards, taste becomes arbitrary.
Without taste, standards become a checklist that can be satisfied mechanically.
Without review discipline, both collapse under speed.

That is why this chapter is not really about style. It is about control. If you are going to use AI in a serious workflow, you need a way to keep output from passing through simply because it arrived quickly and sounded finished.

## What standards actually do

A standard is not a slogan.

It is not “be strategic,” “sound professional,” “make it clear,” or “keep it high quality.” Those phrases are too vague to govern anything.

A real standard makes the work easier to judge.

For example, a standard might require that a recommendation:
- states the decision in one sentence,
- names the main tradeoff directly,
- distinguishes evidence from inference,
- avoids inflated certainty,
- and makes the next action unmistakable.

A standard for a briefing might require that it:
- gives the audience the context they need,
- removes decorative language,
- surfaces the most important uncertainty,
- and keeps ownership visible.

A standard for research notes might require that they:
- separate direct source material from interpretation,
- show what is known versus assumed,
- and preserve enough provenance for later checking.

These are not aesthetic luxuries. They are quality controls.

They matter because generic output often survives precisely where standards are soft. If the only bar is “looks reasonable,” then the workflow will fill with work that is hard to object to and hard to remember.

A strong review system does not rely on vague disappointment after the fact. It names what the output must do before the output arrives.

## The real fight is not against blandness. It is against drift.

The word “generic” can make the problem sound cosmetic.

It is not.

Generic output is dangerous because it creates drift.

A person uses AI to save time. The first-pass output is decent. The review is rushed. The weak phrases survive. The thinking gets lightly compressed into acceptable language. The final artifact does its job well enough. Nothing breaks. Then the same pattern happens again. Over time, the person stops noticing how much of their workflow is now built around the machine’s defaults.

This is how standards erode.

Not through obvious failure, but through repeated acceptance of work that is merely serviceable.

The drift can happen in several ways.

### 1. The work becomes smoother than it is thoughtful
The surface improves while the actual judgment stays thin.

### 2. Review becomes error-checking instead of quality-checking
People look for mistakes, formatting issues, and missing bullets while ignoring whether the output says anything strong, useful, or true enough.

### 3. The median tone becomes the house style
Everything starts sounding equally safe, equally balanced, and equally forgettable.

### 4. Convenience starts deciding what survives
The sentence stays because it is usable. The slide stays because it is already made. The summary stays because no one wants to reopen the thinking.

This is why a fight against generic output is not a fight for decoration. It is a fight against passive acceptance.

The professional task is to keep deciding.

## The wrong way to think about taste

This chapter is not arguing for novelty for its own sake. In most professional settings, the real need is work that is fit for purpose, specific enough to matter, and strong enough not to dissolve into the surrounding noise.

That means taste should be applied through questions like these:
- Does this fit the actual job?
- Does this say something real or only something acceptable?
- What in this output feels generic, inflated, or ownerless?
- What would make a serious reader trust this more?
- What should be removed because it sounds complete without adding substance?

Those are review questions, not artistic poses.

That is the posture to keep.

## A quality-control layer for AI-assisted work

If taste is a review system, then it needs a workflow.

A useful quality-control layer can be built in six moves.

### Step 1. Define what failure would look like before you generate anything
This is the upstream move most people skip.

Before asking for output, write down what would make the result weak.

For example:
- too vague to guide a decision,
- too polished to reveal uncertainty,
- too generic to sound owned,
- too long to hold attention,
- too soft to state the real tradeoff,
- too abstract to be useful,
- or too compressed to preserve meaning.

This matters because bad review often happens when the only prompt is “make it good.” That is not a review standard. It is a hope.

High-agency workflows define failure conditions early so that later review has something real to enforce.

### Step 2. Turn quality into observable criteria
Once you know what weak would look like, define what strong must do.

Ask:
- What must be clear?
- What must feel credible?
- What must remain distinctive?
- What must not be lost in simplification?
- What evidence, logic, or ownership needs to remain visible?

Then write the criteria in concrete language.

For example:
- must state the main recommendation in the opening,
- must keep source certainty visible,
- must remove filler transitions,
- must preserve the sharpest objection,
- must sound calm rather than inflated,
- must include only claims we could defend under questions.

If you cannot judge the output against observable criteria, the workflow will default to taste by mood, which usually collapses under time pressure.

### Step 3. Separate generation from selection
This is one of the most important habits in AI-assisted work.

Generation creates possibilities.
Selection decides what deserves to survive.

Those are different jobs.

Weak workflows blur them together. The first decent-looking version becomes the draft. The draft becomes the artifact. The artifact becomes the official output. No serious selection ever happens.

A stronger workflow treats generated output as material, not verdict.

That means asking:
- Which version is strongest, and why?
- Which version sounds safest rather than clearest?
- What element from version two is better than version one?
- What should be kept from the original human phrasing?
- What should be discarded because it is polished but generic?

The important shift is psychological. Do not meet the model as a passive receiver. Meet it as an editor with options in front of you.

### Step 4. Review in passes, not in a single impression
Many people review AI output by vibe.

They read quickly, feel that it “sounds pretty good,” fix a few lines, and move on.

That is not enough.

A quality-control layer needs distinct review passes. For most professional work, four passes are usually enough.

#### Pass A: Fit
- Does this output actually fit the job?
- Is the audience clear?
- Is the purpose visible?
- Does it solve the problem that was defined?

#### Pass B: Substance
- Does it say anything specific?
- Are the claims meaningful enough to act on?
- Does it separate fact, interpretation, and recommendation clearly enough?

#### Pass C: Standard
- Does it meet the criteria we named in advance?
- Is uncertainty handled honestly?
- Is ownership visible?
- Has anything weak survived simply because it was convenient?

#### Pass D: Distinctiveness
- What sounds generic here?
- What feels borrowed or overly balanced?
- Where has the model flattened tension, voice, or seriousness?
- What sentence, section, or claim still needs to sound more like a responsible human being meant it?

This review sequence works because it prevents stylistic polishing from happening before the more important questions are answered.

### Step 5. Force one comparative judgment
Before finalizing, require one explicit comparison.

Ask:
- Is this version better than the obvious average version?
- What exactly makes it better?
- If I put this next to three other competent outputs, would I still keep it?

This is a simple but powerful check because generic output often survives in isolation. It sounds fine until placed next to other fine-sounding outputs. Then its sameness becomes visible.

A comparative judgment interrupts passive acceptance.

### Step 6. Make one human-owned final pass
The last pass should answer a hard question:

**What in this output is now unmistakably mine to stand behind?**

That may be the framing, the judgment, the recommendation, the prioritization, the interpretation, the final edit, or the refusal to make a claim more confident than it should be.

Whatever the answer is, it should be visible.

If the final artifact feels as though no real person made a meaningful selection inside it, the review system is not finished.

## A representative work scenario

Imagine a manager preparing a short internal brief about a slipping initiative.

They need to explain the current state, make a recommendation, and prepare language that other teams can use without distorting the situation. AI can help with all of this. It can summarize notes, propose outlines, draft alternatives, compress updates, and suggest cleaner phrasing.

In a weak workflow, the manager asks for a polished project update. The model returns one. It sounds calm, reasonable, and complete. It mentions alignment, dependencies, revised timing, and next steps. The manager adjusts a few sentences and sends it.

Nothing in the brief is obviously wrong. But it is generic in all the damaging ways.

It does not say what the real issue is.  
It does not name the tradeoff.  
It hides uncertainty inside professional tone.  
It sounds as if any competent system could have written it.  
It gives readers language without giving them much judgment.

A stronger workflow looks different.

First, the manager defines failure conditions: the brief must not hide schedule risk, must not blur ownership, and must not create false confidence.

Second, the manager defines the quality criteria: open with the actual issue, separate known facts from interpretation, state the recommendation plainly, and keep the next decision visible.

Third, the manager uses AI for option generation rather than immediate acceptance: three possible structures, two tones, one version that is more direct, one that is more cautious.

Fourth, the manager reviews in passes. Fit: does this help the audience decide? Substance: does it name the real problem? Standard: does it preserve uncertainty honestly? Distinctiveness: where does it sound like generic management prose?

### Before / after excerpt

| Generic output | Owned output |
| --- | --- |
| “The initiative is tracking behind plan, but alignment remains strong. Dependencies are being managed and next steps are in progress.” | “The initiative is behind because the decision is still open. This week we narrow scope to protect the date, and I am owning the tradeoff instead of smoothing it over.” |

The same standard shows up in smaller non-writing artifacts too:

| Generic handoff note | Owned handoff note |
| --- | --- |
| “Project is moving forward. Team is aligned, and open items are being tracked for follow-up.” | “Decision still open: cut scope or miss the date. Owner: project lead. Tradeoff: keep full scope or protect the date. Risk: the update goes out before the decision is made.” |

Fifth, the manager forces a comparison and notices that the most polished version is also the least useful because it smooths out the core tension.

Finally, the manager rewrites the recommendation sentence and opening paragraph in human-owned language.

The finished brief may not be flashy. That is not the point.

The point is that it now carries standards.

It tells the truth more directly. It fits the job more precisely. It sounds like it belongs to someone willing to own it.

That is what good taste looks like in practice.

## The same review system has to travel across domains

If this chapter worked only for prose, it would be too narrow for the role Part II needs to play.

The same review discipline shows up elsewhere:
- In **research**, it separates sourced evidence from smooth interpretation.
- In **planning**, it forces tradeoffs, ownership, and recommendation quality to stay visible.
- In **coordination**, it prevents polished recaps from standing in for real alignment.
- In **writing**, it keeps fluency from outrunning point of view.

That cross-domain portability is the real test of standards. If the review system only works in one medium, it is not yet an operating layer.

## Where generic output hides in real workflows

Because the later chapters will go domain by domain, it helps to make the cross-domain pattern explicit now.

Generic output usually hides in one of four places.

### 1. In the opening
The artifact takes too long to say what matters because the language has been optimized for smoothness rather than clarity.

### 2. In the middle
The structure is neat, but the substance is interchangeable. Sections are present because they usually belong, not because they are earning their space.

### 3. In the tone
Everything sounds careful in the same way. Certainty, caution, balance, and authority are all approximated rather than actually chosen.

### 4. In the ending
The output closes with acceptable next steps rather than a real decision, real implication, or real standard.

Once you start looking for these patterns, you see them everywhere.

That is useful because it turns taste from a vague aspiration into something inspectable.

## A practical checklist for standards review

Before generation:
- What would failure look like here?
- What must this output accomplish?
- What would make it generic even if it looked polished?

During generation:
- Am I generating options or accepting a first pass?
- Have I named the criteria strongly enough for the model to be useful?
- What part of the work still needs my judgment rather than more variation?

During review:
- Does this fit the actual job?
- What here is too vague, too safe, or too inflated?
- Where is the output hiding uncertainty or ownership?
- What sentence, section, or claim feels like acceptable average rather than chosen quality?
- What should be cut even if it is technically fine?

Before sending:
- What in this artifact am I actually willing to stand behind?
- If this were compared with three other competent outputs, would I still keep it?
- Does the final version sound like a responsible human selected it, or only like a model completed it?

## The deeper point

The more AI lowers the cost of production, the more professionals need a reliable way to reject what should not survive.

That is what taste and standards are for.

They are not ornamental ideas for people in explicitly creative fields. They are operating requirements for anyone who wants to use AI without letting the machine’s default patterns quietly become the house style of their work.

Chapter 4 defined the work, Chapter 5 built the workflow, and Chapter 6 placed judgment. This chapter adds the final layer: someone still has to judge what is good enough, what is generic, what is misleadingly polished, and what deserves to remain.

That judgment layer is what keeps speed from turning into drift.

It is also what makes the practical chapters possible.

Writing is the first application because it makes generic output painfully visible. It exposes the difference between fluency and thought, between polished language and real authorship, between a draft that looks finished and one that has actually earned the reader’s attention.
