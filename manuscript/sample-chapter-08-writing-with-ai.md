# Sample Chapter — Writing with AI

## Chapter 8. Writing with AI

A few years ago, weak writing was usually slow.

Today, it can arrive polished, grammatically clean, and still not earn the right to exist. AI has made it easy to produce prose that looks finished before the writer has decided what matters.

Writing makes the same tension visible everywhere in this book: speed helps, but judgment still has to choose the shape of the work. AI can break inertia, test structures, tighten language, and help with audience shifts. It can also flatten voice, erase tension, and make a careful idea sound like a brochure.

The question is not whether AI can help you write. It can.

The question is whether you use it to sharpen meaning or merely to accelerate output.

## What weak AI-assisted writing does

The failure mode is familiar even when the draft looks competent:
- it is fluent but uncommitted,
- it is organized but generic,
- it sounds neutral when the real job needs a point of view,
- it lets the writer stop thinking after the first pass.

That is not a model problem. It is a workflow problem.

## Keep the hard sentence with the writer

A useful test is simple: can you still say the hard thing in your own words after the model has produced three decent versions?

Generic sentence:
> We need to align on priorities to stay competitive.

Owned sentence:
> If we keep splitting the team across three launches, none of them will be ready enough to ship with confidence.

The second sentence carries a choice, a cost, and a standard. That is what writing should do.

## A compact workflow for writing with AI

### 1. Define the job before you ask for language
Name the audience, purpose, standard, and constraint in plain language.

### 2. Ask for structure before prose
If the thinking is still forming, use AI for angles, objections, or outlines rather than a full draft.

### 3. Draft the hard paragraph yourself
Use AI to accelerate the parts around the core. Keep the sentence that names the real point in human hands.

### 4. Revise in layers
Check the argument first, then the structure, then the language. AI can help at every layer, but you decide which layer you are fixing.

### 5. Restore specificity
Replace abstract phrasing with concrete detail whenever the writing starts sounding safe instead of true.

### 6. Protect voice deliberately
Write down the editorial rules that matter to you: short paragraphs, no euphemism, no borrowed confidence, no unnecessary polish.

## A real-work scenario

Imagine a strategy lead writing a memo about cutting three features down to one.

A weak draft will sound balanced and lifeless. It will mention priorities, alignment, and customer value without ever saying the uncomfortable part: the team is spreading itself too thin.

A better pass starts with the human judgment:
1. The writer decides the real message: the scope is too wide to ship well.
2. The writer names the emotional reality: some people will hear the cut as a loss.
3. The writer drafts the core paragraph personally.
4. AI helps test three openings, tighten repetition, and cut vague phrasing.
5. The writer does the final courage check: does the memo actually say the hard thing?

You can see the difference in one paragraph:

- Weak version: "To stay aligned with our priorities, we should narrow the roadmap and focus on the items with the highest customer value this quarter."
- Revised version: "We are cutting two of the three features because shipping one workflow that works is better than announcing three that all land half-finished. That will disappoint the teams who pushed for them, but the memo should say that tradeoff plainly instead of hiding it behind 'alignment' language."

That is the right division of labor. The model speeds the revision; the human owns the meaning.

## Where AI helps most

AI is especially useful for breaking inertia, proposing alternate structures, surfacing weak spots and repetition, adapting the same idea for different audiences, and compressing a draft after the core meaning is already clear.

## Where writers get into trouble

The biggest mistake is using AI too early. If you do not know what you mean yet, the model can blur the thought instead of sharpening it.

The second mistake is treating polished language as proof of quality. It is easy to produce something that sounds competent enough to skim. It is much harder to produce something that deserves trust.

The third mistake is outsourcing the mental pressure that writing is supposed to apply. If the draft never forces you to decide, the piece may get faster while your thinking gets weaker.

## A practical checklist for writing with AI

Before drafting:
- What problem am I solving?
- Who is this for?
- What should the reader think, feel, or do differently?
- What must not happen?
- What is at stake if this lands badly?

During drafting:
- Am I asking for structure, language, or both?
- Have I made the key decision myself?
- Does the draft still carry a clear point of view?

During revision:
- Is the argument sharp?
- Is the structure doing real work?
- Is the language alive rather than generic?
- Does this sound like someone I would trust?

## The deeper point

Writing with AI is not just a productivity question. It is a test of whether you still own your own standards.

Use the model to generate, compare, tighten, and adapt.
Do not use it to dodge the difficult part of meaning.

If you keep the hard sentence, the final send, and the standard in human hands, AI can make you faster without making you flatter.
